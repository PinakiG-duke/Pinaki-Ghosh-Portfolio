Research

The central question is how much of the theoretical value of an adaptive decision system is realized when a human acts on its recommendations, and what design choices determine that fraction. Most retail allocation systems treat demand as a forecasting problem. The more fundamental problem is the decision gap — the difference between what an adaptive policy could deliver if allowed to update as uncertainty resolves, and what a static system actually delivers when it cannot.

The Economic Value of Adaptivity (EVA) formalizes this gap and decomposes it into two measurable components: the value of reacting to demand signals within a planning horizon, and the value of anticipating structural demand shifts across horizons. SKUFlux is the four-layer system operationalizing this research across distinct retail demand environments. The human interface layer is the empirical core — grounded in ongoing experimental work under Professor Jana Schaich Borg at Duke.
